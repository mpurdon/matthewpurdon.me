A post with a decent argument and a few paragraphs arrives in the feed, and within moments you find an em dash. The first reaction is "ChatGippity", and the author is dismissed as if a judgement had been delivered.

<Callout variant="quote">You have been weighed, you have been measured and you've been found to have been using AI.</Callout>

The post might be right, wrong, or thought provoking. I don't know, because it is not about what the author was trying to say, it's about _how_ they were saying it. And I am increasingly starting to dislike it. I use AI to collect thoughts over time for an article and then use all of the fragments and ideas to come up with an outline which gets iterated on. Then we (WE?!?!) one-shot a first draft. After that it's edited and messed with by hand in Zed. The site has a style guide, a "voice" for my agentic shadow writer that's been distilled from my Teams messages and emails (not that it could find many emails from me), and a list of words, phrases, and patterns I have banned. I read each sentence and if I even start to think "that sounds like slop" the delete key is immediately and mercilessly applied.

**Consider this:**
> In today's fast-paced digital landscape, the question of AI authorship isn't just a technical curiosity — it's a rich tapestry of trust, authenticity, and perception. As we navigate this evolving frontier, one thing is crystal clear: the way we read is quietly being rewritten. Crucially, this isn't about the words on the page; it's about the story we tell ourselves about who put them there. Let's unpack why this matters. The real question isn't whether a machine wrote it — it's whether we still know how to listen. Ultimately, authenticity is the load-bearing wall of communication, and in a world of infinite content, the message is only as strong as the medium that carries it. Because at the end of the day, the future belongs to those who dare to read between the lines.

I "wrote" that by running the fingerprint checklist backwards, and it is a fine slop specimen. Strip the junk out and the only remaining claim is that perception matters. What I find interesting is how fast your brain turns off. I first noticed it watching YouTube: a creator said "quietly" in the first minute and I had switched to another video before the sentence finished. You most likely did the same with the paragraph above if you even managed to get through it. You did not read it closely, weigh it, and conclude it was empty; you recognised the source, and the reaction followed. That reflex is what this note is about.

## I didn't actually know what it meant until now

Marshall McLuhan was born in Edmonton, spent most of his career at the University of Toronto, and gave us a four-word sentence that gets quoted far more often than it gets explained. "The medium is the message" opens *Understanding Media* (1964), and the usual hot take is that the technology carrying a message reshapes people more than whatever the message says.

I had nodded at that sentence for years without taking it literally. His cleanest example is the electric light, a medium with no content at all, which still rearranged work, cities, and the night without saying anything. His point was that we argue endlessly about what a medium carries and almost never notice what the medium itself is doing to us, because the content is so much easier to have opinions about. What made it click for me was feeling a medium act on me directly. I switched videos on one adverb. The content never got a hearing, because the form had already decided for me.

McLuhan might say I have the wrong kind of message there. A light can carry one in the ordinary sense as soon as people agree on what it means: a porch light left on says somebody is expected, an "on air" light says stay out of the room, and neither spells a word. The adverb worked on me like the porch light. I had learned what it was supposed to mean, and I acted on that.

He wrote that the content of any medium is always another medium: speech is the content of writing, writing is the content of print, print is the content of the telegraph. The content of a model-written post is the author's rough idea, and without the model most of those posts would not exist, because turning the idea into eight hundred clean words was more work than the person who had it was ever going to do.

## The tells are real

The patterns are not imaginary. Models overuse certain constructions, and a post stuffed with them is, more likely than not, going to be something nobody thought hard about. Reading time is finite, and if you don't feel like your argument is worth _your_ time, why is it entitled to any of ours?

That is the part of the reflex I would defend, and it took me a while to see that it is about effort and not about the words. Polish used to be expensive. A clean thousand words meant somebody had spent hours on it, maybe even passed it by an editor, so readers built up an intuition that polish was a rough gauge of how much thought was behind a thing. The model made polish free, and the gauge stopped working. The tells are the replacement gauge: crude, unplanned, pointed at the only evidence left on the page, and unable to tell a post nobody thought about from one where somebody did the work and let the model type.

<Soapbox variant="aside" label="Hot take" signoff="the dash did nothing to you">

Emily Dickinson is the famous case of a writer with a suspect writing style: dashes through most of her poems might get her flagged even though they were written by hand, in a house in Amherst, with no model within a century of her.
</Soapbox>

In one test reported by Decrypt, ZeroGPT rated the Declaration of Independence 97.93% likely to be AI-generated, which would surprise the Continental Congress. A false claim in clean human prose is still false, and a correct claim that a model helped phrase is still correct. My fear is that the reflex is training us to skim by default, and that leaves the argument sitting there, soon to be forgotten.

That being said, human authorship does not settle anything either. People write confident, polished, wrong stuff (been to X lately?) all the time, and always have.

## Whose idea is it

If I argue a point with a model for an afternoon, change my mind twice, throw out its framings, and then have it draft the sentences while I cut half of them, whose argument is that? I think it's mine. The usual way to describe the model's role is as a speechwriter, where the speaker owns the position and answers for it. The comparison breaks on effort. Readers can reasonably care how many hours went into a piece, and "a model produced it in a minute" is information about that, even when the argument survives.

Whether the stigma will fade, I am not sure. My guess is that it does, the way most of these things do, by just being pervasive and ever-present. Normal people that don't interact with LLM output many times per day don't seem to notice it yet. The first people to stop caring might be the ones who were reading for the argument anyway.

## Writing around the suspicion

I have been cutting "quietly" and a dozen other banned words from my own pieces, and I use em dashes far less than I did. Part of that is taste. Part of it is self-defence, because readers take both as evidence of a model, and I can no longer tell which part is which.

Scrubbing the tells fixes the wrong thing. A draft with no "quietly" in it can still be slop, and the guide in this repository, a list of banned words written for a model and obeyed by me, is only the cheap half of the job. The expensive half is the one from [Judgement is the job now](/notes/judgement-is-the-job-now/): somebody has to read what the model produced and decide whether it is true and whether it is worth saying. [Summaries all the way down](/notes/summaries-all-the-way-down/) puts the sin in the same place: not the model, but using it blind, with nobody checking the draft against the source.

You have known since the second paragraph how this piece was made, so if a discount got applied somewhere in the last 1,600 words, I would like to know what it was for. If it was for effort, fair enough; that is what the tells were always standing in for, and I have told you how the hours were spent. If it was for the punctuation, the argument is the same as it was one sentence ago, and nothing about a dash can tell you whether it is right.

<SoapboxFold title="AI detectors are selling horoscopes" signoff="practice, practice, practice">

AI detectors are selling horoscopes. You paste in some text, it hands back a number with two decimal places, and everyone nods like a measurement happened. OpenAI built one of these and pulled it inside six months, because by their own numbers it caught about a quarter of the AI text and called human writing AI nine percent of the time. A [Stanford group](https://hai.stanford.edu/news/ai-detectors-biased-against-non-native-english-writers) ran seven detectors over TOEFL essays written by actual people and the average false positive rate was 61%. People are getting accused of cheating on the strength of this stuff. Jesus wept.

Carl Sagan has a chapter for this. It's called "The Fine Art of Baloney Detection", it's in *The Demon-Haunted World* (a whole book about astrology and its cousins), and two of the questions in it are: has anybody independently confirmed this, and could it be proven wrong? Go ahead and ask the detector.

I mean... our side is no better. Name your agent. Give it a persona. Write the CLAUDE.md, the AGENTS.md, the forty-line prompt somebody on X swears by. This repo has a CLAUDE.md and I can't prove it does a thing; nobody ran that experiment either. It's alchemy. If you actually use these models every day (seriously use them) you find the boring version pretty fast: set your context, keep it tight, and judge what comes back harshly, for the people who are going to read it. Then do it again.
