A post with a decent argument and a few paragraphs arrives in the feed, and within moments someone has found an em dash. The first comment is "ChatGPT", or "AI", and the conversation is dismissed as if a verdict had been delivered.

<Callout variant="quote">You have been weighed, you have been measured and you've been found to have been using AI.</Callout>

The post might be right, wrong, or thought provoking. The comment does not say, because the comment is not about what the author was trying to say, it's about _how_ they were saying it. I am paranoid about this, I use AI to collect thoughts over time for an article and then use all of the fragments and ideas to come up with an outline which gets iterated on. Then we one-shot a first draft. After that it's edited and messed with by hand in Zed. The site has a style guide, a "voice" that's been distilled from my teams messages and emails (not that it could find many emails from me), and a list of words and phrases I have banned. I read each sentence and if I even start to think "that sounds like slop" the delete key is immediately and mercilessly applied.

**Consider this:**
> In today's fast-paced digital landscape, the question of AI authorship isn't just a technical curiosity — it's a rich tapestry of trust, authenticity, and perception. As we navigate this evolving frontier, one thing is crystal clear: the way we read is quietly being rewritten. Crucially, this isn't about the words on the page; it's about the story we tell ourselves about who put them there. Let's unpack why this matters. The real question isn't whether a machine wrote it — it's whether we still know how to listen. Ultimately, authenticity is the load-bearing wall of communication, and in a world of infinite content, the message is only as strong as the medium that carries it. Because at the end of the day, the future belongs to those who dare to read between the lines.

I "wrote" that by running the fingerprint checklist backwards, and it is a specimen. It opens on a "landscape", leans on a "tapestry", tells you what is crystal clear and what is crucial, announces that it is about to unpack something, delivers two reversals and a list of three, drops two em dashes, calls something load-bearing, and finishes on a line built to be screenshotted. It also says nothing. Strip the tics and the only remaining claim is that perception matters. What I find interesting is how fast your brain turns off. I first noticed it on YouTube: a creator said "quietly" in the first minute and I had switched to another video before the sentence finished. That is System 1 in Kahneman's terms, the fast half of the brain that pattern-matches and decides before the slow half gets consulted. You most likely did the same with the paragraph above. You did not read it closely, weigh it, and conclude it was empty; you recognised the source, and the verdict followed. That reflex is what this note is about.

## I didn't actually know what it meant until now

Marshall McLuhan was born in Edmonton, spent most of his career at the University of Toronto, and gave us a four-word sentence that gets quoted far more often than it is explained. "The medium is the message" opens *Understanding Media* (1964), and the usual gloss is that the technology carrying a message reshapes people more than whatever the message says. The railway changed the scale of cities and work whether it hauled coal or commuters. My favourite line from the same book is about the content: it is, he wrote, like a juicy piece of meat carried by the burglar to distract the watchdog of the mind. Everyone is busy with the meat.

I had nodded at that sentence for years without taking it literally. His cleanest example is the electric light, a medium with no content at all unless it is spelling out a shop sign, which still rearranged work, cities and the night without saying anything. His point was that we argue endlessly about what a medium carries and almost never notice what the medium itself is doing to us, because the content is so much easier to have opinions about. What made it click for me was feeling a medium act on me directly. I switched videos on one adverb. The content never got a hearing, because the form had already decided for me.

<Callout variant="note" title="Canadian Heritage, in sixty seconds">
  McLuhan got his own Heritage Minute, which is about as Canadian a credential as exists. <a href="https://www.historicacanada.ca/productions/minutes/marshall-mcluhan" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Historica Canada's version</a> has Cedric Smith playing him in 1961, fascinating a room of students with his ideas about mass media. The medium there was a television spot about a man explaining media.
</Callout>

The AI version runs the other way, and it is a stretch of his idea that he might have objected to. McLuhan's media were channels. A language model is a production process, and what readers react to is a guess about provenance with the style of the text as the only evidence. The shape still matches. The watchdog goes after the carrier and never looks at the meat, so once someone suspects the mechanism, the message stops getting weighed and starts getting filed.

## The tells are real

The patterns are not imaginary. Models overuse certain constructions, and a post stuffed with them is more likely than average to be something nobody thought hard about. Reading time is finite, and if you don't feel like your argument is worth _your_ time, why is it entitled to any of ours?

That is the part of the reflex I would defend, and it took me a while to see that it is about effort and not about the words. Polish used to be expensive. A clean, well-structured thousand words meant somebody had spent hours on it, so readers used polish as a rough gauge of how much thought was behind a thing, the same way effort used to filter bad ideas before anyone built them (I wrote about that in [Judgement is the job now](/notes/judgement-is-the-job-now/)). The model made polish free, and the gauge stopped working. The tells are the replacement gauge: crude, unplanned, and pointed at the only evidence left on the page.

<Soapbox variant="aside" label="Hot take" signoff="the dash did nothing to you">

Emily Dickinson is the famous case of a writer with a suspect writing style: dashes through most of her poems might get her flagged even though they were written by hand, in a house in Amherst, with no model within a century of her. I would pay good money to watch a detector try to grade her.

</Soapbox>

In one test reported by Decrypt, ZeroGPT rated the Declaration of Independence 97.93% likely to be AI-generated, which would surprise the Continental Congress. Style is a weak signal for determining who wrote something and an irrelevant one for whether it is valuable. A false claim in clean human prose is still false, and a correct claim that a model helped phrase is still correct. My fear is that the reflex is training us to skim by default, and that leaves the argument sitting there, unanswered.

That being said, human authorship does not settle anything either. People write confident, polished, wrong stuff (been to X lately?) all the time, and always have.

## Whose idea is it

If I argue a point with a model for an afternoon, change my mind twice, throw out its framings, and then have it draft the sentences while I cut half of them, whose argument is that? I think it's mine, but Carl Sagan's baloney detection kit says to try not to get overly attached to a hypothesis just because it is yours, and I am attached to this one. The usual way to describe the model's role is as a speechwriter, where the speaker owns the position and answers for it. The comparison breaks on effort. Readers can reasonably care how many hours went into a piece, and "a model produced it in a minute" is information about that, even when the argument survives.

<Callout variant="note" title="The baloney detection kit">
  The kit is a chapter of Sagan's *The Demon-Haunted World: Science as a Candle in the Dark* (1995), "The Fine Art of Baloney Detection". It is a list of tools for catching bad arguments, including your own, and I think it is a really great read.
</Callout>

Whether the stigma will fade, I am not sure. Normal people that don't interact with LLM output many times per day don't seem to notice it yet. My guess is that it does, the way most of these things do, by just being pervasive and ever-present. The first people to stop caring might be the ones who were reading for the argument anyway.

## Writing around the suspicion

I have been cutting "quietly" and a dozen other banned words from my own pieces, and I use em dashes far less than I did. Part of that is taste. Part of it is self-defence, because readers take both as evidence of a model, and I can no longer tell which part is which.

Scrubbing the tells fixes the wrong thing. A draft with no "quietly" in it can still be slop, and the guide in this repository, a list of banned words written for a model and obeyed by me, is only the cheap half of the job. The expensive half is the one from [Judgement is the job now](/notes/judgement-is-the-job-now/): somebody has to read what the model produced and decide whether it is true and whether it is worth saying. [Summaries all the way down](/notes/summaries-all-the-way-down/) puts the sin in the same place: not the model, but using it blind, with nobody checking the draft against the source.

You have known since the second paragraph how this piece was made, so if a discount got applied somewhere in the last 1,800 words, I would like to know what it was for. If it was for effort, fair enough; that is what the tells were always standing in for, and I have told you how the hours were spent. If it was for the punctuation, the argument is the same as it was one sentence ago, and nothing about a dash can tell you whether it is right.

<SoapboxFold title="Leave the punctuation alone" signoff="semicolons are next, apparently">

Somebody has to say it: the em dash didn't apply for the job. It's a mark. It's been sitting on the keyboard (and in books, forever) minding its own business, and now it's evidence? Jesus wept. We are suspicious of punctuation now. Punctuation!

I mean... if you actually want to catch slop, stop counting dashes and ask one question: could any sentence in this be wrong? The stuff that is genuinely bad is bad at the first claim. No number, no example, no scene, nothing a person could look at and say "no, that's not what happened". Nothing in it can fail, which is why it reads the same no matter who or what wrote it. That's the tell that has never let me down, and it works on humans too, you know? I've read plenty of fully organic slop, signed and everything.

So go ahead and skim the thing that has nothing to say. Just do it because it has nothing to say, and not because of how it was spelled.
