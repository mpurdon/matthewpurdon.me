<Callout variant="takeaway" title="The glance">
  People now dismiss writing the moment they suspect a model touched it, and they judge the style instead of the argument. Suspicion is a fair prior and a useless rebuttal. The hunt for tells is also changing how humans write, including the ones who never use AI.
</Callout>

A post with a decent argument and a few paragraphs arrives in the feed, and within moments someone has found an em dash. The first comment is "ChatGPT", or "AI", and the conversation is dismissed as if a verdict had been delivered.

<Callout variant="quote">You have been weighed, you have been measured and you've been found to have been using AI.</Callout>

The post might be right, wrong, or thought provoking. The comment does not say, because the comment is not about what the author was trying to say, it's about _how_ they were saying it. I am paranoid about this, I use AI to collect thoughts over time for an article and then use all of the fragments and ideas to come up with an outline which gets iterated on. Then we one-shot a first draft. After that it's edited and messed by hand in Zed. The site has a style guide, a "voice" that's been distilled from my teams messages and emails (not that it could find many emails from me), and a list of words and phrases I have banned. I read each sentence and if I even start to think "that sounds like slop" the delete key is immediately and mercilessly applied.

**Consider this:**
> In today's fast-paced digital landscape, the question of AI authorship isn't just a technical curiosity — it's a rich tapestry of trust, authenticity, and perception. As we navigate this evolving frontier, one thing is crystal clear: the way we read is quietly being rewritten. Crucially, this isn't about the words on the page; it's about the story we tell ourselves about who put them there. Let's unpack why this matters. The real question isn't whether a machine wrote it — it's whether we still know how to listen. Ultimately, authenticity is the load-bearing wall of communication, and in a world of infinite content, the message is only as strong as the medium that carries it. Because at the end of the day, the future belongs to those who dare to read between the lines.

I "wrote" that by running the fingerprint checklist backwards, and it is a specimen. It opens on a "landscape", leans on a "tapestry", tells you what is crystal clear and what is crucial, announces that it is about to unpack something, delivers two reversals and a list of three, drops two em dashes, calls something load-bearing, and finishes on a line built to be screenshotted. It also says nothing. Strip the tics and the only remaining claim is that perception matters. What I find interesting is how fast you knew. You most likely did not read it closely, weigh it, and conclude it was empty; you recognised the source, and the verdict followed. That reflex is what this note is about.

## The line everybody knows

Marshall McLuhan was born in Edmonton, spent most of his career at the University of Toronto, and gave us a four-word sentence that nearly everyone can quote and few can explain. "The medium is the message" opens *Understanding Media* (1964), and the usual gloss is that the technology carrying a message reshapes people more than whatever the message says. The railway changed the scale of cities and work whether it hauled coal or commuters. My favourite line from the same book is about the content: it is, he wrote, like a juicy piece of meat carried by the burglar to distract the watchdog of the mind. Everyone is busy with the meat.

<Callout variant="note" title="Canadian Heritage, in sixty seconds">
  McLuhan got his own Heritage Minute, which is about as Canadian a credential as exists. <a href="https://www.historicacanada.ca/productions/minutes/marshall-mcluhan" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Historica Canada's version</a> has Cedric Smith playing him in 1961, fascinating a room of students with his ideas about mass media. The medium there was a television spot about a man explaining media.
</Callout>

The AI version runs the other way, and it is a stretch of his idea that he might have objected to. McLuhan's media were channels. A language model is a production process, and what readers react to is a guess about provenance with the style of the text as the only evidence. The shape still matches. The watchdog goes after the carrier and never looks at the meat, so once someone suspects the mechanism, the message stops getting weighed and starts getting filed.

## The tells are real

The patterns are not imaginary. Models overuse certain constructions, and a post stuffed with them is more likely than average to be something nobody thought hard about. That is a reasonable prior. Reading time is finite, and an argument that cost nobody effort is entitled to less of yours.

<Soapbox variant="aside" label="Hot take" signoff="the dash did nothing to you">

Emily Dickinson is the famous case of a writer with a suspect writing style: dashes through most of her poems might get her flagged even though they were written by hand, in a house in Amherst, with no model within a century of her. I would pay good money to watch a detector try to grade her.

</Soapbox>

A prior makes a poor verdict, though. In one test reported by Decrypt, ZeroGPT rated the Declaration of Independence 97.93% likely to be AI-generated, which would surprise the Continental Congress. Style is a weak signal for who wrote something and an irrelevant one for whether it is true. A false claim in clean human prose is still false, and a correct claim that a model helped phrase is still correct. "This was written by AI" is a fair reason to skim. As a rebuttal it answers nothing, because the argument is still sitting there, unanswered.

Human authorship does not rescue anything either. People write confident, polished, wrong stuff (been to X lately?) all the time, and always have.

## Writing around the suspicion

I have removed "quietly" from my own published pieces (eleven of them gone, four kept, the four being cases where somebody was literally speaking softly), and I use em dashes far less than I did. Some of that is taste; "quietly" was a crutch and I am better without it. Some of it is self-defence, because readers now take both as evidence of a model, and I notice I can no longer tell the two motives apart.

The models are being steered the same way. People paste lists of banned words into system prompts so the output will pass for human, and the guide in this repository is exactly that list, written for a model and obeyed by me. A person and a machine are now avoiding the same words for the same reason, and the reader deciding whether a sentence is "too AI" is guessing at a boundary both sides are busy moving. Whatever tell is fashionable this year becomes next year's training signal, and the humans who adapted to it are left writing around a fingerprint that has already moved. Nobody planned this. The technology is changing how people write, including people who never open it, which is about as McLuhan as it gets.

## Whose idea is it

If I argue a point with a model for an afternoon, change my mind twice, throw out its framings, and then have it draft the sentences while I cut half of them, whose argument is that? I think it is mine, and I notice that I want that to be true, which is a reason to distrust the answer. The usual comparison is a speechwriter, and it half works: the speaker owns the position and answers for it. It strains on effort. Readers can reasonably care how many hours went into a piece, and "a model produced it in a minute" is information about that, even when the argument survives.

I do not have a clean answer. What I would defend is narrower. When a named person publishes a claim, somebody is accountable for it, and that question can be asked of the claim itself without ever counting its commas.

Whether the stigma fades, I am not sure. My guess is that it does, the way most of these do, by getting boring, and the first people to stop caring will be the ones who were reading for the argument anyway.

You have now read well over a thousand words of this. AI helped me with the outline; the opinions in it are mine. If something shifted just now, a small discount applied backwards to paragraphs you were fine with a minute ago, I would like to know what the discount was for. The argument is the same as it was one sentence ago.

<SoapboxFold title="Leave the punctuation alone" signoff="semicolons are next, apparently">

Somebody has to say it: the em dash didn't apply for the job. It's a mark. It's been sitting on the keyboard (and in books, forever) minding its own business, and now it's evidence? Jesus wept. We are suspicious of punctuation now. Punctuation!

I mean... if you actually want to catch slop, stop counting dashes and ask one question: could any sentence in this be wrong? The stuff that is genuinely bad is bad at the first claim. No number, no example, no scene, nothing a person could look at and say "no, that's not what happened". Nothing in it can fail, which is why it reads the same no matter who or what wrote it. That's the tell that has never let me down, and it works on humans too, you know? I've read plenty of fully organic slop, signed and everything.

So go ahead and skim the thing that has nothing to say. Just do it because it has nothing to say, and not because of how it was spelled.
